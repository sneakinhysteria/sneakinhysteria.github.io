I thought why not start 2021 with a constructive rant. After all, I’m both old and European enough for this. We like a good rant here. I had 3 weeks off, and the final week has provided a long-missing level of energy and clarity that is needed for constructive rants. And the lack of of any other priorities. Blissful.

This post is not about remote best practices. Many have been written and I will gladly add more in the future. From silent meetings to non-linear collaboration, from virtual whiteboards to employee recognition platforms — there is so much good, important content out there. And it’s easy to find. But the resistance towards remote and a lack of understanding of the wider impact is far more fundamental than the How. So here I focus on what drives resistance and why we need to overcome it and take it far beyond business.

This week I caught a tweet my wife was replying to:

And like a couple of boxes falling down a flight of stairs, my thoughts and realisations hit me, collided in flight and piled up at the bottom. Some familiar thoughts on remote work and culture, some new connections.

To state the obvious, many jobs, including many key sectors can’t be done remotely. But many can. And if our work practices had the requirement to be universally applicable, we would be wearing some sort of white coat for work. Fun fact, my first job as an IT assistant at a big insurance company involved a lab coat. In 1998!

I wilfully ignore the agency context of the above tweet. I firmly believe any job that can be done with a laptop, phone, and basic utensils can be done remotely. Particularly in the wider context of Tech. Or as a cat-friendly benchmark, if your job could be done without the use of thumbs, you could probably work remotely. So when we hear people claim it does not or not fully work, there are two basic categories of objections: those coming from all sorts of leadership and individual contributors. And the vast majority, in my own experience, fall into the first category. For this group of people, I think there are a couple of conscious and subconscious drivers:

### The Buzz of Busywork

<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="/content/images/2024/10/0-gqlus-cdyrtnwvta.jpg" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="2000" height="1333"><figcaption>Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@frantic?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com/@frantic?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-creator noopener" target="_blank">Alex Kotliarskyi</a> on&nbsp;<a href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-source noopener" target="_blank">Unsplash</a></figcaption></figure>

We confuse people being paid by the same company, spending a specific time in the same building, talking and using their devices with purpose, collaboration, and communication. It gives us the illusion that things happen, shit gets done. We secretly like when meeting rooms are booked, water cooler conversations are animated and people lean towards screens and point at things. Wonderful. A bit like in the good old Industrial Age, a well oiled factory with all the noise, smells and necessary production waste. Only, it’s a dangerous illusion. But at least it does not require trust or imagination, all the buzz, productive and healthy or not, is right in front of our eyes and we can feel important for facilitating it.

> **Remote maximises real outcomes.**

And I confirm from my own experience that you can create the so called energy, team spirit and enthusiasm remotely. But as a leader, you have to bring more to the virtual table to create it. And as a contributor, you may have to curb cynicism and be more vulnerable.

**Routine —** Pandemic chaos is disrupting our lives and work. Known routines provide order, no matter their risk, cost or impact. Status is also a routine. Office buildings provide status. And if leaders lack he skills to manage remotely, they will desperately try to hang on to what they know. Remote work shapes and demands a leadership style that is much more about the people you lead than about you, and as a result, remote leaders are the better leaders. But the culture you consciously design, create and maintain is much more stable and less elusive than the natural dynamics that ‘just happen’ in an office.

> **Remote maximises leadership.**

<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="/content/images/2024/10/0--jjvpxtnmupwxhlw.jpg" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="2000" height="1333"><figcaption>Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@clemono?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com/@clemono?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-creator noopener" target="_blank">Clem Onojeghuo</a> on&nbsp;<a href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-source noopener" target="_blank">Unsplash</a></figcaption></figure>

**Bias —** As part of the existing routine, we have also grown to listen to those who speak the loudest, most confident and dominant. In-person meetings with verbal real-time contributions strongly favour those who speak loud, are confident and come up with eloquent, quick answers. In-person communication — and as a result — decision forming and making — follows biased dynamics. No surprise that it favours male, white contributors over all other groups. And this is further amplified by the still vastly non-diverse leadership and senior management in our companies. We like people who talk and think like us. And we like people who make decisions like we do. So I assume the following is a common scenario in many companies: ‘I don’t know why they are not onboard. Do they not support the strategy decision? If so, they could have made their point during that meeting, but they didn’t speak up noticeably or interrupted Stuart. So their point obviously wasn’t that important…’.

Filtering out all the voices that don’t sound like you or broadcast through the same channels also denies your company diverse perspectives that mirror the reality outside your privilege bubble. And as is always the case with diversity, inclusion and equality, everyone wins in the end. Bias does also work against those favoured by it.

(Mental note for another post on why all men should be feminists.)

> **Remote maximises diversity, equality and inclusion.**

**Not for everyone?**

But not everyone is happy working remotely, I hear you say. They need the energy from their peers. They get too tempted to do the laundry. They will be distracted by X, Y or Z.

<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card"><img src="/content/images/2024/10/1-gyxhgl4s6ug7tis6msponw-jpeg.jpg" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="1200" height="799"></figure>

I argue that most of these things are not true and those that are, can be designed to work. Design in the sense of consciously creating processes, rituals and habits is the effort we have to make. All this effort will not only enable your team to work remotely, it will vastly improve many other things. And I strongly believe your organisation will benefit from going through this process even if you are not or never go fully remote. Because creating this experience means a manifestation of conscious collaboration far beyond the need to pass handover points in processes.

<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="/content/images/2024/10/0-_6yfoipdbhxnsmxd.jpg" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="2000" height="3000"><figcaption>Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@priscilladupreez?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com/@priscilladupreez?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-creator noopener" target="_blank">Priscilla Du Preez</a> on&nbsp;<a href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-source noopener" target="_blank">Unsplash</a></figcaption></figure>

Many people feel a strong need to connect with others and perceive offices as a place where this happens. So we need to make time for genuine human interaction without offices. And this time has to be ring-fenced against project-related interactions. These moments can’t be productivity-tracked or otherwise repurposed. But tracking the time and frequency of this happening in the right way can be a great KPI for remote culture.

Unless you run a guerillia laundrette from hour home, I also struggle to understand the issue of doing your laundry, cooking lunch, vacuuming your living room or frankly, any other mundane task. This, alongside regular breaks, [actually boosts our creativity](https://www.nature.com/news/why-great-ideas-come-when-you-aren-t-trying-1.10678).

<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="/content/images/2024/10/0-ey4i_r5mwdemitsm.jpg" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="2000" height="1333"><figcaption>Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@mennie?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com/@mennie?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-creator noopener" target="_blank">Mennie</a> on&nbsp;<a href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-source noopener" target="_blank">Unsplash</a></figcaption></figure>

In an office, we hardly spend 100% of our time in the building doing actual work. And if you ask me, the strict and artificial division of actual work vs. personal time is outdated and a relic of the Industrial Age. So when it comes to the amount of work we are allowing or are encouraged to transgress into our lives, it’s important we accept this holistically. This is a deal between two parties. If you wish, you do your laundry, and if you like, you check in on an email after 8pm. It’s a give and take. We need to give people the flexibility to structure their days around their own body and mind clocks. And once this has been clearly communicated and expectations are set, people need to learn to embrace this freedom. This can be a journey but it leads to the entrepreneurial, self motivated and creative talent you always claimed you wanted to hire.

<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="/content/images/2024/10/0-vn_fva_5ciyile6u.jpg" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="2000" height="3000"><figcaption>Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@minhphamdesign?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com/@minhphamdesign?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-creator noopener" target="_blank">Minh Pham</a> on&nbsp;<a href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-source noopener" target="_blank">Unsplash</a></figcaption></figure>

This way of thinking is likely the biggest challenge for leaders over 40. To clock in on time in the morning and to not leave as the first is not a meaningful measure of employee performance or fit. It says a lot more about those who think it is.

  

And our care for people does not stop when they take their laptops home. Remote work requires investment in proper work environments at home. A dedicated room, an ergonomic desk, screens, high quality chairs. Remote work does not mean conducting client meetings from the kitchen counter, but it can if this is the type of customer relationship you have and you are comfortable.

**Why we should really care?**

<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="/content/images/2024/10/0-fcgupy4y0zzs03xi.jpg" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="2000" height="1333"><figcaption>Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@louisap?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com/@louisap?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-creator noopener" target="_blank">Louisa Potter</a> on&nbsp;<a href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" data-href="https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&amp;utm_medium=referral" class="markup--anchor markup--figure-anchor" rel="photo-source noopener" target="_blank">Unsplash</a></figcaption></figure>

Remote is so much more than a business best practice. The ability to do important work with others, no matter where they are based and how they can best contribute goes far beyond business objectives. A world that embraces remote practices builds general crisis resilience.

I couldn’t not believe my own eyes when I saw how officials, politicians, and governments but also media globally started to awkwardly tip-toe around video conferencing last year. Worldwide, we spend a lot of tax-payers money to fund travel of politicians and governments, including security. A world in which governments can function remotely on all local, regional and federal levels means politicians can spend more time being close to those they represent and they in return, can access their representatives better.

And embracing a remote approach further fosters our democracy as it allows grass roots movements and activists to form, gather and grow.

#### We have to start to realise that our location, our mobility, our level of physical or neural ability, our fluency in linear verbal communication are all privileges. If we disconnect the process of achieving results from these factors, we gain the invaluable talent and contribution of a large group of people otherwise unable to access these important processes.

  

And of course, there is the planet and our society. Killing the commute and all its soul and resource destroying side effects, like reducing housing and traffic pressure on cities. Reclaim prime real estate for people. Defuse the toxic business property market. Reduced business travel. This is a key aspect for me but I will keep this short for this post and pick it up separately.

> **Remote maximises humanity.**

* * *

In a world where we rightfully strive for long distance space travel and building human societies on other planets, we better develop a wide-spread natural muscle for a distributed existence and connecting tissue not depending on sharing a physical space. Humanity might depend on it.

**Disclosure:**

I work for a fully remote global company since 2017 and have largely worked remotely over the last 15 years of my career. Since the start of the Covid-19 pandemic\*, I have seen 1 customer contact and 1 colleague face-to-face for a dinner (between Covid waves here in the Netherlands). I miss social face to face meetings as much as most, but collaboration, relationships and business can thrive well in a remote only world. There is no way I would ever accept an office-bound role again.
